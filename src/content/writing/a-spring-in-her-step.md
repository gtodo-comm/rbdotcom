<p><strong>SALEHA KHAN </strong></p>



<p><strong>Age 20, Govandi (Mumbai)</strong></p>



<p>Her father didn’t want her to study after class 8. But with quiet determination she got her way, and paved the way for other girls.</p>



<p><strong>Saleha&#8217;s Journey</strong></p>



<p>Saleha grew up in Govandi, a part of Mumbai where humans co-exist with flies, filth and faeces.</p>



<p>“My father has a cycle repair shop, my mother is a housewife… we are originally from U.P. ”</p>



<p>This is an area which the town – and its planners – has conveniently forgotten. For its residents are low-income, migrant families. And by the way, we need to dump our garbage somewhere… so let’s do it here. That dumping ground has grown and grown into what is, literally, a mountain of filth. Saleha recalls playing in the dumping ground, as a child.</p>



<p>“We didn’t know it was a dirty place… my friends used to climb up and down the heaps of garbage and pick up expired bags of chips.”</p>



<p>Once Saleha’s mother caught her with one such packet and snatched it from her, with a scolding. She warned the little girl not to go there and that eating such things would make her sick.</p>



<p>“Don’t go there again,” she warned. “<em>Bachchon ke liye theek nahi hai</em>”. (It’s not good for children.)&nbsp;</p>



<p>But where else could the children jump and run around? Like any other slum in Mumbai (but a few degrees worse!) Bainganwadi was cramped and overcowded, with narrow lanes and overflowing gutters. Why, quite often, the gutter water flowed right into Saleha’s tiny one-room house which she shared with 6 family members.</p>



<p>“It was difficult… there was no toilet in our home, or even a water connection.”</p>



<p>The slum-dwellers had to buy water from tankers, at a cost of Rs 30 to Rs 50 per gallon. The buckets would have to be hauled back to the home over quite a distance. Everyone was surprised to see Saleha – with her small build – helping her mother with this daily chore. And, she always did it with a smile.</p>



<p>“<em>Bachpan se hi Saleha ka bahut helping nature hai</em>,” says her mother. (From childhood, Saleha has the desire to be of help to others.)</p>



<p>Unlike most girls of her age, Saleha was also very keen to attend school. The government school in Govandi is only upto class 7. The secondary school is in Chembur, which&nbsp;is quite some distance away. Saleha’s father was not keen to send her there. He had never been to school himself and didn’t really see the value of education.</p>



<p>“<em>Hamesha padhti rahegi kya</em>?” (What’s the point of studying all the time?)</p>



<p>Saleha’s elder sister had already dropped out of school, she stayed home helping out their mother. It took quite a bit of convincing for abbu (father) to relent. The local school teacher came to their home and counselled him, as well. Finally, it was Saleha who came up with a workable plan. She convinced 3 other girls from the neighbourhood to join the same school.</p>



<p>“We will all travel together –&nbsp;<em>ek doosre ka khayal rakhenge</em>!” (We will keep each other safe.)</p>



<p>Although soft-spoken and respectful, Saleha has always had a mind of her own.</p>



<p>One morning, social workers from the NGO Apnalaya had come to her home, to inform them about menstrual hygiene classes. Saleha’s mother forbade her from joining.&nbsp;</p>



<p>She thought her daughter – the age 12 – was ‘too young’ for this adult information. After all young girls get all kinds of ideas and are often led astray…</p>



<div><span aria-hidden="true"></span><div>
<p>“Although soft-spoken, Saleha has always had a mind of her own.”</p>
</div></div>



<p>Although her mother did not grant permission, Saleha decided to go anyway. And she found the class very useful and interesting. The instructor explained what is menstruation, how to use a pad, how to maintain hygiene during periods. And it was all done in the form of discussion and games.</p>



<p>“<em>Class mein achcha achcha batate hain</em>,” she told her mother. (They teach us positive things.)</p>



<p>In fact, after about a year, Saleha felt she knew enough to take a class herself. Though a bit nervous in the beginning, she found her confidence.</p>



<p>“I felt really happy when girls came and told me these classes were helping them!”&nbsp;</p>



<p>After scoring 59% in the 10th board exams, Saleha was able to join a junior college closer to home, along with her&nbsp;friends. Meanwhile, she got an opportunity to take self defence training through an NGO called Aangan. They taught girls how to face attackers by using martial arts and even chilli powder! </p>



<!--nextpage-->



<p>“<em>Kabhi real mein use kiya nahi&nbsp;</em>(Never had to use it in real life) but I felt I can face any situation!”</p>



<p>It was around this time that Saleha’s small acts of courage came to the notice of the Sakaal newspaper group, which has instituted the Savitribai Phule award. This award is usally given to an older woman working towards a social cause. But they were so impressed by 17-year-old Saleha, that they shortlisted her name.</p>



<p>For the very first time, her humble household was visited by a journalist. Who went back satisfied&nbsp;<em>ki story mein dum hai&nbsp;</em>(it’s a true and inspiring story).</p>



<p>The award ceremony was held on 1st January, 2017 and the organisers had just one request.</p>



<p>“Please bring your father with you to attend the function in Ulhasnagar (a suburb of Mumbai).”</p>



<div><span aria-hidden="true"></span><div>
<p>“Sometimes parents shut their doors when we go to meet them but we keep following up until they agree to hear us out.”</p>
</div></div>



<p>After a great deal of persuasion, Shakil Khan agreed to accompany his daughter. He was astounded to see a huge crowd at the venue. And they were all clapping and cheering for Saleha.</p>



<p>“<em>Aaj mujhe bahut khushi hai&nbsp;</em>(I am very happy today),” he said. “You are no less than a boy for me.”</p>



<p>This event became a turning point. Saleha’s father now became her biggest supporter. When she completed her 12th and wanted to join a commerce college in Sanpada, which was at quite a distance from their home, he did not scold her, or raise any objection. In fact, he willingly bears as much expense as he possibly can, the rest is covered by a fellowship from the NGO Apnalaya.</p>



<p>Along with her studies, Saleha continues to take sessions on menstrual hygiene for girls (she has completed over 250 so far!). She also undertook a unique project to highlight the poor level of sanitation in the slum. Having learnt photography at a workshop organised by Save the Children, she decided to put this skill to good use.</p>



<p>“We took photographs of our area… the toilets, the garbage.. and organised an exhibition.”&nbsp;</p>



<p>Filthy latrines, piles of muck and buzzing flies were indeed a common sight. But the residents had grown immune to it. But seeing those images blown up in full colour, HD, stirred something inside them. Women decided to monitor the hygiene in the common toilets and make sure they were cleaned at least twice a day. And slowly, there was visible change.</p>



<p>Things have improved for Saleha Khan and her family. She now lives in a 3-room vertical house with running water. The gutter outside her house has been fixed, and here too she has played a role. By persuading her mother and other women in the neighbourhood to sign a petition and deliver it to the local MLA.</p>



<p>“<em>Humne apna haq maanga aur wo humein mil gaya</em>.” (We demanded what was our right and got it.)</p>



<p>Fighting these battles is not easy – and more so for a young girl. Platforms like Ashoka Youth Venture played a role in building Saleha’s confidence. When she attended the training session in Bangalore in 2017, she realised that there were so many other girls from all over India who had faced issues related to education, just like she had.</p>



<p>“Meeting these girls gave me strength… I thought I must be active and do good for others.”</p>



<p>In September 2018, Saleha Khan was chosen as a #GirlChampion, who would speak on behalf of the girls of India, at the United Nations General Assembly session. As she boarded the Air India flight to New York City, she was both excited and nervous. It was her first time on an airplane and her first time in a foreign country.</p>



<p>At the forum, Saleha participated in discussions on ‘Sustainable Development Goals’. She was thrilled to meet children like herself, from 155 countries. A film on education of the girl child was shown, in which Saleha’s struggle was covered. When Melinda Gates spoke, she specifically mentioned the brave young girl from Mumbai. But she couldn’t pronounce Saleha’s name correctly.</p>



<p>“So after her speech I went and introduced myself… that I am Saleha, not Sally.”</p>



<p>“Oh… I apologise for that,” said Melinda Gates. “I am so happy to meet you in person.”</p>



<p>Saleha returned to Mumbai energised and determined to ‘do more’. One of the things she resolved to do was ‘improve her English’. This would also help in her goal of doing a Master’s in Social Work in Tata Institute of Social Sciences (TISS). Meanwhile, she continues to juggle her final year in college with giving tuitions to earn some extra income. And working with Apnalaya.</p>



<p>Saleha is a mentor for 14 girls in her community. She has the quiet credibility and authority to counsel parents to do what’s right for their daughters. Let them go to school, not hurry to get them married. Not go through the trauma faced by her own sister – who had a baby at 16. Because she didn’t know any better back then.</p>



<p>“<em>Ab to mere pappa bhi kehte hain… galti ho gayi, phir aisa nahin honey denge</em>.” (Even my father agrees, what happened with my sister was wrong and it should not happen again.)</p>



<p>This is change. Real and sustainable change. Change from within the community.</p>



<p>Going a step further, Saleha’s mother adds, “If we keep our girls in purdah and let the boys roam around freely without any checks, that is not right either.”</p>



<p>Both boys and girls must be sensitised on how to deal with their bodies, and with each other. So, these days&nbsp;<em>ladkon ki training bhi hoti hai&nbsp;</em>(there is training for boys too). The girls use a ‘Paro’ kit while the boys use ‘Shankar’ kit. When they recently interacted with celebrity Twinkle Khanna, both genders were present. And no one was embarrassed to talk about periods.</p>



<p>“<em>Bahut acchha lagta hai</em>…” Saleha beams. (It’s wonderful to see this.)</p>



<div><span aria-hidden="true"></span><div>
<p>“For a girl was seen as a burden, while a boy was treated like a king. Now we forge a new equation, after every winter… there is spring!”</p>
</div></div>
