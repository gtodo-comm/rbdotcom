<h2>Meena Bindra, Biba</h2>



<p><em>At the age of 39, a navy wife and mother of two started a business to earn some pocket money. With the help of her two sons she turned the humble salwar kameez into a national brand with annual sales of Rs. 300 crore.</em></p>



<h2>Rashmi&#8217;s Perspective of Meena</h2>



<p>Millions have heard of Ritu Kumar, but few own a ‘Ritu Kumar.’</p>



<p>Few have heard of Meena Bindra, but millions wear her clothes.</p>



<p>Biba is India’s largest readymade ethnic-wear brand, with over 100 stand-alone stores across the country. And it all began in 1982, when Meena started a business for pocket money, with an eight-thousand-rupee loan.</p>



<p>I meet Meena at her unassuming corporate office tucked away in Delhi’s Chhatarpur area. She sits behind a large desk, wearing a stylish black kurta and cream-coloured stole.</p>



<p>Meena could be your mother or my mother – women who grew up in an India where girls were educated, but never thought of a ‘career.’</p>



<p>“I got married at 19,” she says, “and I spent the next 20 years raising a family.”</p>



<p>It was at the age of 39 that she got into designing of clothes and selling them informally.</p>



<p>Working from home, without targets, without a business plan. But Meena had her pulse on what women wanted to wear – stylish, affordable Punjabi suits.</p>



<p>“I was lucky to be in the right place at the right time,” she shrugs. “I never had to go out and do any marketing.”</p>



<p>Buyers and retailers lined up at her doorstep to place orders for readymade suits.</p>



<p>And yet, it wasn’t easy. Meena’s husband was a naval officer with a transferable job. This meant staying in separate cities for 7 years, for the sake of the business. How would she manage it all alone?</p>



<p>A good mother runs a home with co-operation. Meena ran her business on similar principles – with the co-operation of her sons.</p>



<p>Elder son, Sanjay, took over the commercials, expanding Biba from a cottage industry to an industrial scale. Younger son, Siddharth, took it to the next level, with stand-alone Biba stores.</p>



<p>Together, they have built a Rs. 300 crore ethnic-wear empire. It sounds too good, too easy to be true.</p>



<p>But that is what life can be, if you see the possibilities and not the obstacles.</p>



<p>That is what life is meant to be, for you.</p>



<!--nextpage-->



<p>Meena Bindra was born and brought up in Delhi.</p>



<p>“I grew up in a large family of six siblings – three brothers and three sisters. I was somewhere in the middle.”</p>



<p>Meena’s father was a businessman, but he passed away when she was only 9 years old. However, he left behind a lot of property so her mother was able to provide and give them a ‘normal childhood.’</p>



<p>After completing a BA in History from Miranda House, Meena got married. She was nineteen-and-a-half and in love.</p>



<p>“My husband was in the Indian Navy and almost ten years older than me.”</p>



<p>As a navy wife, Meena moved around all over the country – from Delhi to Bombay to Vishakhapatnam. Never more than three years in one place.</p>



<p>“My son, Sanjay, was born in 1965, Siddharth came in 1974. So the first 20 years of marriage, I was just busy looking after home and family.”</p>



<p>Only when her kids had grown up, did Meena think of ‘doing something.’&nbsp;</p>



<p>“My elder son was in boarding school, the younger one in school. Playing cards was not my scene and I also knew I am not cut out for a job!”</p>



<p>What Meena did enjoy was designing clothes.</p>



<p>“I hadn’t done a formal course, but I dabbled with prints and colours. I got a few of my saris block-printed, just for fun.”</p>



<p>But to go into business, you need <em>some </em>money.</p>



<p>“I spoke to my husband and he arranged for a loan from Syndicate Bank.”</p>



<p>Eight thousand rupees is not a lot of money, but enough to buy some fabric and hire a tailor. But as they say, fortune favours the brave.</p>



<p>“My first lucky break was when someone introduced me to a block printer called Devesh. He was a young boy, passionate about his work and he had a big factory.”</p>



<p>Every morning, Meena would take a cab to the factory and spend the day there, experimenting with various techniques and colour combinations.</p>



<p>“We enjoyed experimenting with new techniques of printing. Tie and dye, <em>khari </em>printing, whatever we did came out really well!”</p>



<p>Through trial and error, Meena put together 40 salwar suits – all casual-wear and reasonably priced (under Rs. 200). Some stitched, some unstitched.</p>



<p>“I had a little sale in my house and everything got sold. I also got a lot of orders!”</p>



<p>This first sale resulted in a small profit of Rs. 3000. With that money, Meena bought fabric for 80 suits and that too sold out quickly.</p>



<p>“So that really encouraged me, and the same money which I made, I would take it, buy more fabric and make more suits!”</p>



<p>It was a business, and yet not strictly business.</p>



<p>“I lived in a huge flat which was like an ‘open house.’ <em>Koi chai pee raha hai, koi </em>coffee <em>pee raha hai</em>… It was a very easy atmosphere where women enjoyed coming, sitting and chatting.”</p>



<p>And they would also try and buy. Knowing that in case I go home and don’t like it, Meena will take it back.</p>



<p>“It was not such a commercial venture, actually,” says Meena. “The buyers were my friends, first and foremost, not customers.”</p>



<p>Purely by word of mouth, Meena’s suits became ‘famous’ among ladies in the Colaba and Cuffe Parade areas of Bombay. By the end of the year, she had three tailors doing jobwork and started getting enquiries from retailers like Benzer and Sheetal.</p>



<p>“Once I started supplying outside, I needed a name for my bill book. I decided on ‘Biba’.”</p>



<p>Retailers placed ‘large’ orders – 100 pieces at a time. And, they wanted new designs and a wider range of fabric.</p>



<p>“I got into terrycots and silks, and quickly scaled up the production,” smiles Meena.</p>



<p>From a time pass and hobby venture, Biba was quickly becoming a <em>real </em>business. Driven by a force bigger than the creator herself.</p>



<p>“I never did any marketing as such, but I think maybe the time was right. New shops were opening, they needed to stock readymade <em>salwar kameez </em>and they heard about me… so I got big orders.”</p>



<p>But Meena has only hazy memories of what exactly she earned in that first couple of years.</p>



<p>“I was doing well, but I cannot recall exactly… not lakhs, but definitely I earned in thousands.”</p>



<p>Thousands quickly did become lakhs because, in 1986, three years into business, Meena moved into a 1000 sq ft office at Kemp’s Corner. An office which was paid for entirely by money earned from Biba.</p>



<p>And yet, there was no business <em>plan</em>, as such.</p>



<p>“I was just flooded with orders and had also opened my own boutique in Kemp’s Corner.”</p>



<p><em>“Jo ban raha tha bik raha tha </em>– so there were no targets, no deadlines, I never felt I am under any pressure.”</p>



<p>Things might have continued in this happy-go-lucky manner, except that, around this time, Meena’s elder son, Sanjay, completed his BCom and joined the business.</p>



<!--nextpage-->



<blockquote>
<p><strong>“I wanted to keep busy and earn some extra money, pocket money.</strong> <strong>That’s why I started designing clothes.”</strong></p>



<p>“I didn’t encourage him initially. I said – you don’t know anything about <em>salwar kameez </em>and you don’t know anything about business! First go do an MBA and then I’ll think about it.”</p>



<p>But Sanjay would not take no for an answer.</p>



<p>“I would say, even from his side, initially it was not so serious, but once we shifted to the office he got into Biba full-time.”</p>



<p>And he proved to be a big asset. Sanjay quickly took over the ‘boring’ side of the business – handling the labour, taking orders, keeping accounts. Now, Meena could focus wholely and solely on designing the clothes.</p>



<p>The next few years, Biba grew at a steady pace. More range, more outlets – not just in Bombay, but all over India. Retailers from as far as Bangalore and Jaipur came and placed orders.</p>



<p>By 1993, Biba had become one of India’s largest ethnic-wear wholesalers, selling 1000-2000 pieces every month.</p>



<p>“I think our turnover at this point was Rs. 8-10 crore&#8230; (<em>shakes her head</em>). No, at that time we were selling wholesale so it would be less. It must have been around Rs. 2 crore.”</p>



<p>There was money in the business, but it was not the main driving force.</p>



<p>Meanwhile, there were other forces acting in Meena’s favour. By the mid-90s, India’s first multi-city department store, Shopper’s Stop, came into being. They too came to Biba for ladies’ ethnic- wear. In the process, Meena learnt many lessons.</p>



<p>“We were forced to become more professional – to stand by our commitments, deliver on time and also bring our costs down, without compromising on quality.”</p>



<p>It wasn’t easy. From the very beginning, Biba had outsourced its manufacturing.</p>



<p>“When faced with a production problem, my first reaction used to be, ‘What can I do? My tailors are like this only’!”</p>



<p>But then, it was a problem Meena would have to solve. Advance planning, control systems and quality checks helped tailors become more efficient. Sanjay handled most of this work.</p>



<p>“I can’t say I was 100% involved, but I was not 100% not involved – it was a joint thing.”</p>



<p>In 1993, Biba had around 10 employees and worked with around 100 tailors. The tailors worked in groups of 10 or 20, and many of these units produced exclusively for the company. This introduced a measure of accountability.</p>



<p>“Once we were giving 500 pieces to one tailoring unit, we would ask for a date of delivery.”</p>



<p>If the delivery date was near and tailors were falling behind, they would be asked to work night-shifts. But scaling up to meet demand was, and still remains, a challenge.</p>



<p>“For men’s shirts you can have an assembly line. For <em>salwar kameez</em>, 5-6 different <em>karigars </em>may need to work on one piece.”</p>



<p>What’s more, the fabric itself is handmade, not mill-made. So it is not standardised.</p>



<p>“If I order 1000 metres of a particular print from Jaipur, it comes in five different shades. So how do I fulfill a bulk order?”</p>



<p>Working with limitations and yet going beyond limits is the true test of any entrepreneur. And Biba passed that test with flying colours. By the year 2000, production had scaled up to 5000 pieces per month.</p>



<p>Demand was never an issue – as Shopper’s Stop and then Pantaloons opened new outlets, they needed more and more stocks.</p>



<p>“We told our tailor masters, ‘We have more work for you. Why don’t you keep more staff?’ so they grew along with us and were happy.”</p>

<p>While tailors expected to be paid in cash, the stores expected credit. But the credit period was 30-45 days and there was generally no delay. So Biba could manage its cash flows without bank limits or overdrafts.</p>



<p>“We never took any outside funding as such. I don’t know if that was the right thing – we could have grown faster, perhaps.”</p>



<p>The turning point for Biba came when her younger son, Siddharth, joined the company after graduating from Harvard in 2002. Although by then Biba had a wide footprint and annual revenues of over Rs. 25 crore, it was not a well-known brand name.</p>



<p>“Siddharth had a very clear vision – we must have our own retail outlets.”</p>



<p>Biba opened its first company-owned outlet in 2004, at In Orbit and CR2 malls in Mumbai. Both shops did remarkably well from day one, with sales of Rs. 12-15 lakh per month.</p>



<p>“That encouraged us and we started booking shops wherever we thought a good mall is coming up. Automatically we get footfalls.”</p>



<p>Of course, this kind of expansion requires management bandwidth and funding. These aspects were handled by Siddharth. In fact, the whole company was restructured and, in 2006, Kishore Biyani bought a 10% stake in Biba for Rs. 110 crore.</p>



<p>“Our growth since 2004 has been phenomenal,” admits Meena.</p>



<p>In March 2012, Biba’s annual revenues stood at Rs. 300 crore, with 90 company-owned outlets contributing 50% of sales. The company continues to outsource manufacturing, but employs around 1000 people in supervisory roles and for retail sales.</p>



<p>A long, long way from a business started to earn some ‘extra pocket money.’&nbsp;</p>



<p>“I never imagined it when I started… but as you grow, your vision keeps growing. Now, I feel we can grow to any height, even become a global brand.”</p>



<p>But did getting into business mean a compromise in personal life? Meena maintains she was able to achieve that fine balance.</p>



<p>“When I first started, I never ever worked after 6 pm. My husband, being in the navy, we had a lot of evening engagements.”</p>



<p>The problem Meena faced was that her husband had a transferable job. When he was posted to Delhi, she stayed back in Bombay – and he was always supportive.</p>



<p>“I would spend 10 days in Delhi, rest in Bombay. We lived like this for 8-9 years, until his retirement in 1993.”</p>



<p>Meena then moved to Delhi while Sanjay stayed on in Bombay.</p>



<p>“I had bought a flat at Worli Seaface. Sanjay got married and moved there and I started an office in Delhi.”</p>



<p>Meena’s deep and continuing commitment is towards great design. “I rely on my own sensibilities – simple, elegant, wearable design.”</p>



<p>Even today, with Biba producing 60-70,000 pieces a month, and professionals to handle all aspects, the one thing Meena oversees herself is designing.</p>



<p>“We have a design team, but I still give the brief, I go through the colours. Samples are made and I give the final approval.”</p>



<p>Of course, designs are worked on at least a year in advance. And once samples are approved, a made-for-scale, scientific process takes over. And Meena is happy about that.</p>



<p>“I don’t like dealing with too many people and you know I’m not such a good administrator. Frankly, if I had to handle all that, I would not have wanted to grow so much.”</p>

<p>Working with family, she feels, has been a blessing. Because you can trust them. And whatever you are building, you are ultimately doing it with and for your family.</p>



<p>“Of course, it requires certain amount of adjustment,” she smiles.</p>



<p>Meena’s husband was never interested in the business. Even after retirement, he was busy with consulting projects and authoring a book. He passed away in 2011.</p>



<p>“I think I was happier not having him in the business,” she admits.</p>



<p>With her sons, Meena had a clear demarcation of boundaries. Yet, there was argument and friction.</p>



<p>“I thought Sanjay was very radical, he thought I wasn’t willing to change. But we always worked things out. As a mother I would eventually give in.”</p>



<p>The dynamics changed when Siddharth also joined the business.</p>



<p>“There were differences of opinion on how to take the business forward,” says Meena. “Ultimately, they decided to work separately.”</p>



<p>In 2010, Sanjay sold his stake and has started a new ethnic-wear label called ‘Seven East.’</p>



<p>“We are still close as a family,” says Meena. “In fact, it’s better this way because conflict is just a waste of energy.”</p>



<p>Energy is the force which moves mountains and working mothers.</p>



<p>“I do yoga, pranayama, walking and swimming to keep my energy up!”</p>



<p>And then there are deeper dimensions. A journey within, which, for Meena, began 22 years ago.</p>



<p>“I came across a book called <em>I Am That </em>by Nisarga Dutta Maharaj. I didn’t understand much of it in the beginning, but I started thinking about questions like ‘who we are’ and ‘what is the purpose of life?’ ”</p>



<p>Meena read and re-read the book, until the meaning started sinking in. She later bought all the other books by the same author and devoured them. Even today, <em>I Am That </em>occupies pride of place on her bedside table.</p>



<p>“It’s not something that you can just read 5-6 pages, just half a page at any given time. But over time it has changed me – made me a better and calmer person.”</p>



<p>A person who does not blame others because each one is playing their role in life. So you accept the world as it <em>is</em>, not as it should be.</p>



<p>And enjoy all the blessings you have.</p>



<p>“I don’t enjoy cooking, but I love having a beautiful house and am very fond of interiors, gardening and meeting friends.”</p>



<p>All things bright and beautiful, all pleasures great and small. A woman can be a wife, a mother and an entrepreneur.</p>



<p>Live a dream and have it <em>all</em>.</p>

<h2>Advice to Women Entrepreneurs</h2>



<p>If you passionately want to do something, then do it. Every woman has the potential and capability, you must never feel <em>‘hum toh nahi kar sakte’</em>.</p>



<p>Women are naturally attracted to women-oriented products. We all wear clothes and jewellery, we all eat good food. So it is easier to start a business in such areas.</p>



<p>I have no formal training, either in business or in designing, I had no money and my husband was in a transferable job. So, it seems like this, all the odds were against me, you know, but then, I took the plunge.</p>



<p>Yes, it requires a lot of work discipline. You can’t say <em>ki aaj kar kiya, kal nahin kiya</em>. There were times when I found it too much, but then I had commitments, I had to honour them. So you have to be focused.</p>



<p>Working with family members is a good way to grow your business. I would recommend it to all women.</p>
